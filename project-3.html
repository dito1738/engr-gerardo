Iterative Process & Tolerances

As expected in rapid prototyping, our first iteration required refinement to achieve a proper fit.

Iteration 1: The Fit Test

Upon printing V1, we discovered our tolerance was too tight. The PCB mounting pegs were exactly [X]mm, matching the holes, leaving no room for the plastic's thermal expansion. Furthermore, the wheel clearance caused slight friction.

Iteration 2: The Final Fit

For V2, we returned to OnShape and applied a clearance tolerance of [+0.4mm] to the mounting holes. We also expanded the wheel arches by [X]mm. The resulting print snapped onto the electronics perfectly.

5. Subtractive Manufacturing: Laser Cut Chassis

After finalizing the 3D print, we pivoted to Subtractive Manufacturing. This required a completely different design philosophy: translating 3D constraints into flat 2D planes.

Before cutting our final design, we both completed a practice laser cut to ensure we understood the safety protocols and focus calibration for the machine.

To design the chassis in OnShape for the laser cutter, we utilized a [parametric finger joint box design / layered acrylic stack design]. We had to account for the kerf (the width of the laser beam's cut) to ensure our joints press-fit together without glue.

Figure 4: Assembled laser-cut chassis.

6. Final Reflection: Additive vs. Subtractive

This project highlighted the distinct challenges between Additive (3D printing) and Subtractive (Laser Cutting) manufacturing.

3D Printing offered immense freedom for complex geometry (like the curved wheel arches and custom snap-fits), but print times were long, making iteration slow. Tolerances had to account for plastic shrinkage and layer squish.

Laser Cutting, conversely, was incredibly fast, allowing for rapid iteration. However, the design process was mentally harder, as we had to figure out how to enclose 3D electronics using strictly flat 2D planes and kerf-adjusted finger joints. Ultimately, learning to measure with calipers and design around the physical constraints of real-world electronics was a highly rewarding engineering challenge.
